## Full voiceover

> On this plateau in Indonesia, the dew freezes into ice. But the ground is boiling. Same place?
> In 1979, one morning, more than 140 villagers here died. No burns. No blast hit them.
> Let's check the science. Starting right under our feet.
> It's 6,371 km to the center of the Earth. The geothermal wells here go down about 2 km. Shrink Earth to an apple, and that wouldn't even get through the skin. Barely scratching the surface, and it's already this hot?
> Normally, the ground heats up about 25 to 30 degrees for every kilometer you go down. But this plateau sits on an old volcano. The magma is shallow. So the magma is the stove. Rainwater soaks into porous rock. That's what's in the pot. On top, a layer of sealed rock. That's the lid. Stove, water, lid. This place is Earth's pressure cooker.
> Under that lid, the water gets hotter than 250 degrees and stays liquid, because it's squeezed. The craters are the valves. Wells tap the steam, and it spins turbines for electricity. That's why Indonesia is the world's number two geothermal power producer.
> But that still doesn't explain 1979. A pressure cooker doesn't just release steam. It releases gas. The rotten-egg smell is sulfur. It makes you run. The most dangerous one has no smell at all. Carbon dioxide. It's heavier than air, so it flows downhill and pools in the valleys.
> 1979 wasn't a cursed crater. It was a gas that sank.
> So this place isn't weird. A fridge on top, a pressure cooker underneath.
> Visiting a crater? Stay on the trail, stay out of the dips, don't linger.
> Be honest… ever taken a selfie way too close to a volcano crater? Tell me in the comments.

(±290 words → ±105–110 s at a relaxed narration pace)

## Notes for a global audience
- "Dieng" is shown as a map label, not leaned on in the voiceover. Viewers outside Indonesia don't know the name, so the VO says "this plateau in Indonesia".
- Temperatures are in °C. If the target is mostly US viewers, add °F on screen as a small third line (−2°C = 28°F, 90°C = 194°F, 250°C = 482°F).
- The Indonesian-only CTA ("Kawah Sikidang") is replaced with a universal one ("a volcano crater").

## Fact check

| Claim | Status | Note |
|---|---|---|
| "Dieng has the largest geothermal capacity in the world" | **False, not used** | Dieng is ~60 MW (Unit 1), with a 55 MW Unit 2 under construction. The world's largest field is The Geysers, California (~1,500 MW). |
| Indonesia is the world's #2 geothermal power producer | Verified | ~2,740 MW installed at the end of 2025, behind the US (~3,900 MW) |
| Sinila 1979 death toll | Uncertain → "more than 140" | Sources give 142, 149, or 175–180. "More than 140" holds for all of them. |
| Main gas was CO₂ | Strong | A peer-reviewed study says nearly pure CO₂; some sources also mention H₂S. Saying "no smell" refers to CO₂ only. |
| Frozen dew on the plateau | Strong | Happens in the dry season (±July–August); −2°C is illustrative |
| Sikidang water ±90°C | Strong | At ~2,000 m, water boils at ~93°C |
| Wells ~2 km, apple analogy | Checked | 2/6,371 ≈ 0.03%; on a 4 cm apple that's ~0.013 mm, thinner than the peel |
| Normal geothermal gradient 25–30°C/km | Strong | Continental-crust average |
| Reservoir >250°C | Strong | Literature puts Dieng's reservoir at ~250–330°C |
| "Pressure cooker" | Analogy | Simplifies a hydrothermal system (heat source, reservoir, caprock, surface vents) |

Sources: ThinkGeoEnergy (Dieng Unit 2; global top 10 at year-end 2025), Global Energy Monitor wiki (Dieng plant), Wikipedia (The Geysers; 1979 eruption of Sinila crater), Antara News (Indonesia ranks second).
